Every fall, billions of birds pour south along the East Coast, most of them at night and almost all of them unseen. This atlas follows that river: who passes through the Northeast, what sets them moving, how they find the way, and when to look up.

The flyway

A river with many sources

The Atlantic Flyway is less a road than a watershed. Its headwaters lie across the whole north: Alaskan spruce, Arctic tundra, the hayfields of Vermont, a New Jersey salt marsh. In late summer the streams begin to run. Shorebirds leave the Arctic in July; warblers trickle out of the boreal forest in August; by October the flow is at its height, and on the biggest nights weather radar has counted more than a billion birds migrating over the United States in a single night.

Where it goes depends on the bird. Some stop in New Jersey for the winter. Some keep to the land and follow the Appalachians to Texas and Mexico. Others make for the edge of the continent and leap: across the Gulf of Mexico, across the Caribbean, or straight out over the Atlantic toward South America. Pick a species below and scrub through the season to watch its river move.

About 4 billion birds cross south over the US–Canada border each fall. About 4.7 billion cross the southern border, because the count now includes this year’s young raised in the US.

The shaded half of the globe is where it is night right now, by your clock. Turn the globe to find it: that is where the songbirds are flying.

Why leave

Chasing a long day

A migrant’s year is a trade. The north offers a short, extravagant summer: a flush of insects, long hours of light to gather them, and room to nest away from the crowded tropics. A pair of warblers feeding nestlings in Quebec in June has nearly 16 hours of daylight to hunt in. Then the insects vanish, the ponds freeze, and the same forest that fed them would starve them.

So they move with the sun. The heavy line in Fig. 2 shows what that means for a Red Knot. By breeding in the Arctic and wintering at the tip of South America, its days never fall much below 12 hours: it skips the dark half of the year in both hemispheres.

Not every bird leaves. Chickadees, cardinals and most woodpeckers stay put and switch diets. Migration pays only for birds whose food disappears.

An inner calendar

Caged warblers kept under unchanging light still grow restless at night, twice a year, for years. The rhythm runs a little shorter or longer than twelve months, which shows it comes from inside the bird. Germans named the night fidgeting Zugunruhe, “migration restlessness.”

Day length

Shortening days in late summer set that calendar to local time. They trigger hormonal changes that bring on the restlessness, a new appetite and, in many species, a molt into fresh feathers before the trip.

Fuel

Migrants overeat on purpose, a state called hyperphagia, and store fat at astonishing rates. A Blackpoll Warbler on the Massachusetts coast can nearly double its weight in a couple of weeks before it leaves. Fat is the densest fuel an animal can carry.

The weather

With the calendar set and the tank full, the wind gives the final signal. In fall, the big nights follow cold fronts: clearing skies, falling temperatures and a north or northwest wind at their backs.

The night shift

Most of the river flows after dark

At least 80 percent of North America’s migratory bird species travel at night. Thrushes, warblers, sparrows, cuckoos and many shorebirds feed by day and fly by night. The air is cooler and steadier, so they lose less water and fight less turbulence. Hawks are asleep. The stars are out to steer by. And the day is left free for eating.

Songbirds lift off within about an hour of dusk, climb to between 500 and 2,000 meters, and cruise at about 20 to 35 miles an hour. Weather radars built to track rain see them as a soft green bloom that swells over each station after sunset. The most birds are aloft two to four hours after dark. Toward dawn they come down to rest and feed, often wherever they happen to be, which is how a city park fills with warblers overnight.

On a quiet fall night, step outside and listen: the high “seet” of warblers, the “queep” of Swainson’s Thrushes. Recording these flight calls is now a branch of science.

Migrants sleep in snatches: dozens of brief naps by day, some of them with one eye open.

Finding the way

Four compasses and a map

A bird making its first migration has never seen its destination. What it inherits is a program: a direction to fly and roughly how long to keep flying. Young Blackcaps raised by hand still face the way their parents’ population migrates, and the young of crosses between two populations split the difference.

To hold that direction, birds read several compasses and check them against each other. Adults also build something richer, a sense of where they are, which lets them correct course when the wind throws them off. Each panel below is one instrument.

Sunset matters. In experiments, Savannah Sparrows recalibrated their magnetic compass against the band of polarized light in the twilight sky.

The ridge

Hawks ride the mountain’s wind

For a big hawk, flapping is expensive, so it travels on borrowed energy. When a northwest wind strikes the long wall of the Kittatinny Ridge, the air is forced upward along its face. A hawk can hang in that updraft and glide southwest for miles, barely flapping. The ridge runs about 185 miles across Pennsylvania and another 40 through New Jersey, and it is the reason hawk watches line its crest.

When the wind dies, the sun takes over. By late morning, fields heat faster than forests, and bubbles of warm air rise from them. Broad-winged Hawks find these thermals, circle up inside them in groups called kettles, then peel off and glide to the next one, losing height until they find more lift.

A soaring hawk spends far less energy than a flapping one. The trade is time: no lift, no travel. Hawks wait out rain and calm mornings.

The coast

Where the land runs out

Migrants travel on a broad front, but the shape of the land squeezes them. Northwest winds push night migrants toward the coast. At dawn, birds that find themselves over the ocean turn back to land, so barrier islands and peninsulas fill up after a good night. Hawks that will not cross open water follow the shore south until it ends.

Cape May is the classic case. Everything moving down the Jersey shore arrives at a point with Delaware Bay on one side and the Atlantic on the other. Falcons and songbirds stream past in October; many hawks turn north along the bay to look for a shorter crossing. In May the same bay feeds Red Knots on horseshoe crab eggs. Cape May’s best hawk day on record: about 21,800 on October 4, 1977, more than half of them Sharp-shinned Hawks.

The calendar

The season, bird by bird

Fall migration in the Northeast lasts nearly half the year. It starts in July, when adult sandpipers that failed to nest or finished early are already back on the mudflats. It ends in December with geese, eagles and owls. Long-distance migrants go first; birds wintering close by go last. The order is so regular that birders plan vacations around it.

Spring is faster. Birds race north to claim territory, and half of the season’s night traffic over New York City passes between about May 7 and May 22.

    Helping

    A thinner river

    The river is running lower. A 2019 study estimated that North America has nearly 3 billion fewer birds than in 1970, a drop of 29 percent, and grassland birds and migrants have lost the most. Migration is the riskiest part of a bird’s year: lit buildings draw night migrants off course, glass kills them in the morning, and the stopover patches they need are paved one at a time.

    Turn lights off

    During migration, March to mid-June and mid-August through November, turn off or shield outdoor lights overnight, or at least from 11 pm to 6 am. BirdCast publishes nightly forecasts. New York’s Tribute in Light now goes dark for about 20 minutes whenever more than 1,000 birds are circling in it.

    Mark the glass

    Birds see reflected sky and trees, not windows. Dots, tape or cord on the outside of the glass, spaced no more than two inches apart, stop most collisions.

    Plant for the stopover

    Native oaks, cherries, willows and goldenrods feed the caterpillars and berries migrants need to refuel. A yard can be a fuel stop.

    Keep cats inside

    Outdoor cats are among the largest human-linked causes of bird deaths in the United States, and tired migrants on the ground are easy prey.

    Count what you see

    Checklists in eBird, flight-call recordings and hawk watch counts are how the timetables on this page were built. Every count adds to the record.

    Visit a hawk watch

    Raccoon Ridge, Montclair, Hawk Mountain and Cape May welcome visitors through the fall. The counters are glad to point out what is passing.
