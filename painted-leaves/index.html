I · Late June to September

It starts with the length of the night

The turning begins months before anyone notices it. After the June solstice the nights grow longer, and by late summer the trees of the Northeast are already getting ready to shut their leaves down.

field noteWeather changes from year to year. The sun’s schedule does not, which makes night length the steadiest signal a tree can read.

Day length appears to set the start. In a long study of a European aspen, the breakdown of chlorophyll began within the same four-day window in September in seven of eight years, warm or cold. Temperature set the pace: the green faded by 2 to 6 percent a day, about 4 percent on average, and two to three times faster in cold autumns.

The trigger is still argued over. Later work from the same Swedish lab concluded that day length alone does not start the process, and some researchers now think trees simply stop rebuilding chlorophyll as days shorten and nights cool. Either way, the calendar and the thermometer work together.

II · Inside the leaf

A leaf holds three paints

Two are there all summer. The third is mixed fresh in the fall.

green · chlorophyll

The worker

Captures light for photosynthesis. Trees build and break it down all summer. In fall the building stops.

yellow & orange · carotenoids

The hidden one

Present since spring, masked by chlorophyll. When the green goes, the yellow is simply revealed.

red & purple · anthocyanins

The new one

Absent from summer leaves. Made in autumn from sugar trapped in the leaf as it shuts down.

brown · tannins

The last word

Always in the leaf. They show at the very end, giving oaks and beech their bronze and tan.

The green retreats

The green retreats. Chlorophyll is worth taking apart. Up to three quarters of the nitrogen in a leaf cell sits in its chloroplasts, much of it in the proteins that hold chlorophyll in place. Before a tree drops a leaf, it dismantles that machinery and moves the nutrients back into its twigs and roots for next spring. In a global analysis of leaf nutrients, deciduous trees reclaimed close to 60 percent of their leaf nitrogen and phosphorus. Aspen recovers about 90 percent of its nitrogen.

by the numbersPlants worldwide take apart roughly a billion tons of chlorophyll every year.

Loose chlorophyll is dangerous in sunlight, so the breakdown runs in a strict order. One enzyme pulls the magnesium atom from the center of the molecule, another clips off its long tail, and a third, pheophorbide a oxygenase, splits the ring open. The pieces end up colorless and are parked in the cell’s vacuole.

Yellow was there all along. Carotenoids help harvest light and shield the leaf from too much of it. A summer leaf holds roughly four to six times more chlorophyll than carotenoid, so the green wins. In autumn the balance flips. At Harvard Forest, chlorophyll fell about 93 percent while carotenoids fell only about 54 percent. The gold of a birch, hickory or aspen is pigment that was in the leaf in July.

Red is made new. Anthocyanins are built in autumn, in the vacuoles of the leaf’s upper cell layer, from sugars that stay in the leaf as it shuts down. They appear once chlorophyll has dropped below about 20 µg/cm², around the midpoint of the change.

Because sugar is the raw material, anything that traps sugar in the leaf makes it redder. When researchers cut a ring of bark around sugar maple branches, the share of red leaves on normally yellow trees rose from about 12 to 54 percent. Chilling branches without wounding them raised leaf sugars two- to tenfold and anthocyanins by about as much. Sugar maples short on nitrogen turn red earlier and more completely.

Why make a new pigment in a leaf that is about to fall? The leading idea is sunscreen: the red layer shades the leaf’s machinery while nutrients are still being pulled out. Plants that cannot make anthocyanin recovered less nitrogen under stress, and red dogwood leaves recovered from bright-light stress that left yellow leaves damaged. Other explanations, including antioxidant protection and a warning signal to aphids, have both supporters and critics. The question is open.

“How beautiful, when a whole tree is like one great scarlet fruit full of ripe juices…”Thoreau on the red maple, “Autumnal Tints”

Red is also a regional habit. About 70 percent of the woody species at Harvard Forest make anthocyanin in the fall, while only about one in ten temperate tree species worldwide turns red. When Jasper Cropsey showed his Autumn on the Hudson River in London in 1860, British viewers doubted the colors were real, so he mounted American leaves beside the canvas.

Brown comes last. Tannins sit in the leaf all year. They show as everything else fades, which is why oaks and beech finish the season in russet, bronze and tan.

III · The last weeks on the branch

How a tree lets go

While the pigments change, a narrow band of small cells forms across the base of each leaf stalk. As the leaf ages it sends less of the hormone auxin down the stalk, and the cells in that band become sensitive to ethylene. Enzymes then loosen the cell walls until the leaf hangs by little more than its veins, and a breeze finishes the job.

On the tree’s side, a layer of corky cells seals the wound before the leaf falls, leaving a leaf scar just below a bud. That bud, with next spring’s leaves folded inside, formed back in summer.

marcescenceDead leaves that stay on through winter. Beech was the last species to drop at Harvard Forest, with half its leaves down by about November 2.

Some trees never finish. In many oaks and young beeches the separation layer does not complete, and the dry leaves rattle on the branches until spring pushes them off.

IV · Late September to mid November

The color moves south and downhill

Color starts in the far north and on the high ridges and works toward the coast. Forecasters put the pace at roughly 50 to 100 miles a week, and trees above 3,000 feet peak 10 to 14 days ahead of the valleys below them. From the first scarlet in Aroostook County to the last oaks near Cape May, the wave takes about six weeks to cross the region.

How the map is drawn

Typical peak dates come from a simple model tuned to state foliage reports: peak arrives about 3.5 days earlier per degree of latitude north, about 5.5 days earlier per 1,000 feet of elevation, up to about two weeks later beside the ocean, and a little later near the Great Lakes. Spruce and fir in the north woods and on the high peaks, and pitch pine in the Pine Barrens, stay green.

Real seasons run early or late by a week or more, and a single hillside can differ from the next.

VI · Good years and dull ones

Weather writes the details

The order of the trees barely changes from year to year. How bright they get, and how long they last, depends on the weather of the late summer and fall.

  • best

    Warm, sunny days with cool nights that stay above freezing. Sun makes sugar, and cool nights slow its export, so more of it becomes red pigment.

  • dry, not parched

    “Dry but not drought” is the ideal, in the words of Penn State’s Marc Abrams. Severe drought brings early, muted color and early leaf drop, as in Pennsylvania in 2019.

  • warm and gray

    A warm, cloudy autumn delays the peak and dulls the reds.

  • hard freeze

    An early hard freeze can stop red pigment cold. Researchers disagree on how much a light frost helps.

  • wet summer

    Long periods of wet leaves invite leaf-spot fungi. Anthracnose spotted sugar maples across northern New England in the wet summer of 2023, and the leaves were dull and fell early.

  • storms

    Wind and heavy rain at peak strip the trees and cut the season short.

Maine · Sept 3 to Oct 7

Dry summer, bright leaves

Portland had its second-warmest summer on record, and southern, coastal and eastern Maine ran 9 to 17 inches short of rain over twelve months. By Oct 7 the north and west were past peak with heavy leaf drop, and the state reported the colors as phenomenal.

Maine Drought Task Force; Maine DACF foliage report

A statewide Drought Warning is in effect. Extreme drought in part of south Jersey in August eased to moderate drought by late September, with temperatures above normal.

Poconos · Oct 8 to 14

Peak in the north

Northern Wayne County is at peak with about 90 percent color. The central Poconos should peak Oct 10 to 17 and the southern zone Oct 20 to 28.

Pocono Mountains Visitors Bureau

A later fall

Peak color in New England now arrives as much as a week later than it did in the 1950s, according to Colby College ecologist Amanda Gallinat. In Vermont, the University of Vermont’s Alexandra Kosiba puts the shift at about a week over thirty years, with fall nights in Burlington roughly 4.5 °F warmer than in 1970.

why laterWarm Septembers slow the loss of chlorophyll, and the cool snaps that speed the change arrive later in the year.

A study of eight species at Harvard Forest found that warm Septembers delay the color, and projected later color and leaf fall for most species through 2100. In that projection the color season of sugar maple and black gum grows by about a day per decade, while white ash loses about three (Archetti et al. 2013).

VII · Painters of the turning

Holding on to October

American painters made autumn a national subject in the middle of the 1800s. The Hudson River School climbed into the Catskills and Adirondacks each fall to sketch, then finished large canvases in the studio, sometimes far from the trees. Cropsey painted Autumn on the Hudson River from memory, in London. Later, painters like Winslow Homer took watercolor into the woods themselves.
